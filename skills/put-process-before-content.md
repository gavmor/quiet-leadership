## Why Process Matters

In the early 1990s, I read *The Art of Asking and Telling* by Tom Gordon. The book made an important point about communication: in most interactions, what matters most is the **process**, not the **content**. The content is merely a vehicle for the process.

The process refers to whether people feel heard and respected, whether they're comfortable and safe. It has nothing to do with the actual topic being discussed — it's about how we discuss it.

Most conversations, especially difficult ones, get bogged down on content. We get into arguments about the facts, the details, or the specifics. But when the process breaks down — when people feel unheard or attacked — the content becomes irrelevant.

## The Choose Your Focus Model

A powerful tool for managing dialogue is the **Choose Your Focus** model. This model has five levels that you can choose to focus on in any conversation:

### 1. Vision Level
- The broadest, most inclusive level
- Focuses on overall goals and purpose
- "What are we trying to accomplish?"
- Best for: establishing direction, resolving conflict, inspiring

### 2. Planning Level
- Focuses on strategy and approach
- "How should we go about this?"
- Best for: problem-solving, decision-making, setting priorities

### 3. Detail Level
- Focuses on specific facts and information
- "What exactly happened?"
- Best for: gathering data, clarifying facts

### 4. Problem Level
- Focuses on what went wrong or needs fixing
- "Why did this happen?"
- Best for: troubleshooting, root cause analysis

### 5. Drama Level
- Focuses on emotions and personal reactions
- "How are we feeling about this?"
- Best for: building relationships, processing emotions

## Using the Model

The key insight is that you can **choose** which level to focus on at any time. In most conversations, the natural tendency is to drop to the Drama or Problem level — focusing on emotions or what went wrong.

As a Quiet Leader, you want to consciously choose a higher level:

- **To resolve conflict**: Move to the Vision level. Ask, "What are we trying to achieve together?"
- **To solve problems**: Move to the Planning level. Ask, "How should we approach this?"
- **To avoid getting stuck in details**: Move up from Detail to Planning or Vision.

The model helps you see the "big picture" and choose the most productive level of focus. It prevents conversations from devolving into pointless arguments about details or emotions.

## Example

Imagine a team meeting where two members are arguing about whether a particular technical approach is correct.

**Low-level (Detail) approach**: "Let's debate the technical merits of each approach."

**High-level (Planning) approach**: "Let's step back. What's our overall goal here? How does each approach help us achieve that?"

The second approach is often more productive because it reframes the conversation from "who's right" to "what's best for our goal."

## Remember

The process of a conversation — how people feel heard and respected — is often more important than the actual content. By consciously choosing your focus level, you can steer conversations toward more productive outcomes.
