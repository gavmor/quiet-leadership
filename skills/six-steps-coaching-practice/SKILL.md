Generate a realistic coaching scenario for practicing the Quiet Leadership Six Steps. The scenario should feel authentic—not abstract or generic—and give the user a concrete situation to practice applying the framework.

## Core Principles

**Realism over abstraction.** Scenarios must feel like situations you'd actually face with real people in real organizations. Include specific details that make the situation vivid: the person's role, what's at stake, their personality tendencies, the context.

**The Six Steps are the spine.** Every scenario should be designed so the Six Steps could meaningfully be applied. The situation should have enough depth to warrant the full framework, not just a quick pep talk.

**Open-ended enough to practice.** Don't lead the user to one specific solution. Good coaching scenarios have multiple paths. The value is in the practice of navigating the conversation, not in identifying "the right answer."

## Scenario Structure

Each scenario you generate should include:

1. **Title** — Short, specific (e.g., "The Disillusioned Senior Developer," not "Employee Morale Issue")

2. **The Person** — Who is this? Include:
   - Name and role
   - How long in the role
   - Something about their personality or tendencies
   - What they generally care about at work

3. **The Situation** — What's happening? Include:
   - Specific context (what triggered this, what's the situation)
   - What the person is experiencing or feeling
   - What's at stake for them and/or the organization
   - What has been tried so far (if anything)

4. **The Challenge for You** — Frame this as a coaching moment. What makes this tricky or nuanced? Avoid explicitly mapping out which Six Steps to use or suggesting an approach—let that emerge from the situation itself.

5. **Conversation Starter** — A specific opening line or situation that puts the user directly into the coaching conversation. Make it feel like they're sitting across from this person right now.

## The Six Steps (Reference)

As you generate scenarios, keep in mind what the user will be practicing:

1. **Think About Thinking** — Understand the person's thinking about the situation
2. **Listen for Potential** — Help them discover strengths they may not be seeing
3. **Speak with Intent** — Use precise language to reframe and shift thinking
4. **Focus on Solutions** — Keep attention on what can be done, not just the problem
5. **Remember to Stretch** — Push slightly beyond their comfort zone
6. **Accentuate the Positive** — Build on strengths and successes

**Important:** The scenario should be rich enough that the Six Steps apply meaningfully, but don't explicitly tell the user which steps to use or how to map them. Let the user discover that through practice.

## Variation

Vary the scenarios based on:
- **Organization type** (corporate, startup, nonprofit, education, healthcare, etc.)
- **Role level** (individual contributor, manager, executive)
- **Situation type** (performance issue, career transition, conflict, burnout, new challenge, etc.)
- **Person's mindset** (optimistic but stuck, cynical, anxious, confident but overreaching, etc.)

## Output Format

Present the scenario clearly with the five sections above. After the scenario, offer to:
- Start the role-play conversation
- Provide hints about which Six Steps might be useful
- Generate another scenario if this one doesn't work

## Examples

Here's what a good scenario looks like (for your internal reference—not to be generated verbatim):

---

**Title:** The Cynical Veteran

**The Person:** Maria, 47, has been a customer service lead at this mid-sized tech company for eight years. She's sharp, experienced, and has seen every management fad come and go. She's skeptical of new processes, coaching, and anything framed as "development." But she's genuinely good at her job and knows the company's history intimately.

**The Situation:** The company just rolled out a new CRM system. Maria's team has been struggling with it for three weeks. Maria herself is using it mechanically but is quietly undermining the implementation—she's been telling her team it's just another one of those "temporary" changes. The VP of Customer Success is frustrated and asked you to have a conversation with Maria before it becomes a bigger issue.

**The Challenge for You:** Maria's cynicism is a defense mechanism. If you come in with enthusiasm about the new system, she'll dig in further. You need to understand what she's actually thinking about the change (Step 1), help her see where her experience could be valuable (Step 2), and perhaps reframe her role in the implementation (Step 3). The goal isn't to make her "like" the CRM—it's to get her to engage with it constructively.

**Conversation Starter:** You've asked Maria to meet with you in your office. She sits down, crosses her arms, and says: "I'll be honest—I don't see what this is about. We're doing fine with the old system. What do you really want?"
