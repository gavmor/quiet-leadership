# Scenario: The Overly Responsible Engineer

## The Person

David Chen, 34, is a senior backend engineer at your mid-sized SaaS company. He's been with the team for four years, promoted to senior two years ago. David is meticulous, technically strong, and genuinely cares about the product's reliability. He's also quiet, tends to internalize stress, and has a history of taking ownership beyond his scope. His peers respect him but sometimes find him hard to read.

## The Situation

Over the past six weeks, several incidents have occurred in production. A database migration went wrong and caused a two-hour outage. A third-party API change broke a key integration. A junior developer deployed untested code that corrupted some customer data.

After each incident, David has volunteered to handle the post-mortem documentation, owned the remediation work, and in meetings has framed each failure as something he should have caught. He's staying late to patch issues, rewriting documentation, and has started second-guessing every deployment. He's not complaining out loud, but he's visibly tired, his code review turnaround has slowed, and he's become withdrawn in team discussions.

You noticed this pattern intensifying and want to have a conversation before it tips into actual burnout or causes him to step back from his work entirely.

## The Challenge for You

David's pattern of taking blame isn't just about these incidents—it's a deep-seated way he operates. If you simply tell him to "relax" or "share the responsibility," he'll nod and continue because he genuinely believes he should be carrying this. A directive approach could backfire, making him feel like you don't understand the weight he's feeling.

This requires understanding his actual thinking about responsibility and failure (Step 1), helping him recognize his genuine strengths without reinforcing the over-responsibility (Step 2), and possibly reframing what "good leadership" looks like in his role (Step 3). You'll need to focus on sustainable patterns rather than just fixing this moment (Step 4), gently push him to consider a different approach than what's comfortable (Step 5), and anchor the conversation in his clear value to the team (Step 6).

## Conversation Starter

You've asked David to meet with you for a casual one-on-one this morning, framing it as a check-in. He arrives, looking slightly haggard, with a coffee cup. He takes a seat and says:

"Just to be upfront, I've been thinking about the API integration issue. I feel like I should have caught that dependency change before it hit production. I'm going to set up a monitoring check for that going forward."
