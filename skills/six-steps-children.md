# Using the Six Steps with Children

> [!tldr] The Six Steps can help children and teenagers develop better thinking skills. Focus on fostering their interest rather than coercing them, and help them think through their own problems and decisions.

## Focus Follows Interest

Tim Gallwey noted: "Focus is the quintessential component of superior performance in every activity, no matter what the level of skill or the age of the performer. Focus follows interest, and interest does not need coercion."

The same is true for adults. The Six Steps work best when people are genuinely interested in exploring their thinking.

## Working with Teenagers

Many otherwise intelligent adults fall in a heap when it comes to interacting with their teenage children. So "Can I use these principles with my kids?" is a question often asked.

The answer is: yes and no.

### The "No" Part
When people ask this, they are often really asking: "Can you give me any tricks for controlling my teenager?" For these parents, the Six Steps aren't really designed for control — they're designed for developing better thinking in the child.

### The "Yes" Part
If your goal is to help your child think better about their own life, problems, and choices, then the Six Steps are very useful.

## Example: A Parent and a Failing Child

A parent came to David Rock with a problem: their child was failing school and wasn't interested in studying. Using the Six Steps:

1. **Think about thinking**: "What do I actually want for my child?" — Not that they get good grades, but that they develop into a responsible adult who can think for themselves.
2. **Listen for potential**: "What's actually going on with my child? What are their interests?"
3. **Speak with intent**: "What would you like to be doing with your time?"
4. **Dance toward insight**: Through conversation, the child discovered they liked the idea of being a doctor because they liked helping people.
5. **Create new thinking**: "What if you connected your studies to becoming a doctor? What would that look like?"
6. **Follow up**: Over time, the child began to study more because they had a real reason, not because they were forced.

## When to Use This Skill

- A child is struggling with school
- A teenager is making poor decisions
- You want to help a child develop problem-solving skills
- You're dealing with a rebellious teenager
- Any situation where you want to help a child think more clearly about their own life
