# Focus on Solutions

> [!tldr] Shift attention from problems to solutions by asking "what" instead of "why" questions. Focusing on problems embeds problems in the brain; focusing on solutions creates new pathways for success.

## The Problem vs. Solutions Focus

The language we use determines how people experience situations and what solutions seem possible.

**Problem-focused questions:**
- "Why didn't you hit your targets?"
- "Why is the team behind schedule?"
- "Why did that meeting go wrong?"

**Solution-focused questions:**
- "What do you need to do to hit targets next time?"
- "What can be done to get the team back on schedule?"
- "What could you do differently in that situation?"

## Why "Why" Is Dangerous

"Why" questions have a specific linguistic structure that leads the brain into a particular pattern of thinking:

1. **Backward-looking** — They direct attention to past causes
2. **Fault-finding** — They imply there is a flaw or deficiency to identify
3. **Fixed frame** — They assume the problem is embedded and permanent
4. **Self-critical** — They invite people to judge themselves harshly

"Why" questions often lead to defensive, self-critical thinking. People feel bad about the past and see themselves as fundamentally inadequate.

## The Science Behind Solution-Focus

David Rock's neuroscience research provides a compelling explanation for why solution-focused questions work better:

- **Focusing on problems embeds problems** — When you dwell on what went wrong, you reinforce the neural pathways associated with that failure
- **Focusing on solutions creates new wiring** — When you imagine success, you strengthen pathways that make future success more likely
- **Solution questions are future-oriented** — They direct attention toward what can be accomplished, not what was lost

In a corporate context, this means that when leaders focus on what can be achieved rather than what went wrong, they help people envision and work toward success.

## How to Apply This Skill

### Step 1: Listen for Problem Language
Pay attention to when someone is using backward-looking, problem-focused language. They may be dwelling on past mistakes, assigning blame, or expressing hopelessness.

### Step 2: Reframe with "What"
Gently redirect the conversation from problems to solutions:

**Instead of:** "Why did you miss the deadline?"
**Try:** "What do you need to ensure you hit the deadline next time?"

**Instead of:** "Why is there a conflict on your team?"
**Try:** "What could you do to improve the situation with your team?"

### Step 3: Explore Possibilities
Once you've shifted to solution-focused language, help the person explore options:
- "What are some possible approaches?"
- "What would it look like if this worked out?"
- "What resources or support might help?"

### Step 4: Focus on the First Step
Help the person identify a concrete first step:
- "What's one thing you could do this week?"
- "Who could you talk to about this?"
- "What information do you need?"

## Example: Reframing a Team Problem

**Employee:** "My team is falling apart. Everyone is angry. We're not meeting any deadlines."

**Problem-focused response:** "Why is everyone so angry? What did you do?"

**Solution-focused response:** "What can you do to help people feel better and get the team back on track?"

The solution-focused response invites the employee to think about actions and solutions rather than dwelling on blame and past failures.

## When to Use This Skill

- An employee is complaining about a problem with no apparent solution
- Someone is dwelling on past mistakes or failures
- A conversation is stuck in a blame cycle
- You need to help someone see possibilities rather than obstacles
- An employee feels stuck or hopeless about a situation

## Relationship to Step 1

This skill works in tandem with [[step-1-think-about-thinking]]. Both focus on how people experience and interpret events:
- **Step 1** helps people examine their basic mental structures (beliefs, assumptions, definitions)
- **Focus on Solutions** helps people shift from problem-embedded thinking to solution-oriented thinking

Both skills leverage the neuroscience insight that how we frame a situation determines what we experience and what solutions seem possible.

## Key Phrases

Use these to shift toward solution-focus:
- "What could you do about that?"
- "What would help?"
- "How could this work out?"
- "What's the next step?"
- "What's one small thing you could try?"

## Common Mistakes

- **Accepting problem language** — Don't let people dwell on problems; gently redirect
- **Using "why" accidentally** — Watch for yourself slipping into problem-focused questions
- **Being too quick to suggest solutions** — Help people find their own solutions through questions
- **Ignoring feelings** — Acknowledge emotions before shifting to solutions
