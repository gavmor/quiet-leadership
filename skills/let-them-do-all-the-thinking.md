# Let Them Do All the Thinking

> [!tldr] People should make their own connections. You can't put new thoughts into someone's head; the most effective way to change thinking is to create conditions where the person discovers the connection themselves.

## The Core Principle

The most important principle in the whole of Quiet Leadership is this: **let people make their own connections**.

We all know how we want people to think. It seems natural to say, "Think like I do." But that rarely works. The reason is that people change their thinking not when they're told what to think, but when they make the connection themselves.

Without this approach, nothing that comes after it will help. This is the foundation upon which all other Quiet Leadership skills rest.

## The Ladder of Approaches

There's a ladder of approaches, ranging from 100 percent self-directed thinking on the part of the listener (most effective) to having the answer provided by the speaker (least effective):

1. **The listener makes the connection themselves** — Best. Most durable change.
2. **The listener is helped to make the connection themselves** — Still very effective.
3. **The answer is provided by the speaker in a useful way** — Least effective, but sometimes necessary.

## Three Specific Approaches

### 1. Help Them Make the Connection on the Spot

Ask questions that help the person make the connection right then. For example, instead of telling someone they need to talk to a client, ask: "What would happen if you spoke directly to them about this?"

This keeps the thinking in their head where it belongs.

### 2. Help Them Make the Connection Later

Sometimes people can't make the connection right away. Give them something to think about and return to it later. This might be a question posed after the conversation, a follow-up email, or a suggestion to reconsider the issue after some reflection.

### 3. Provide the Answer in a Useful Way

When necessary, give the answer directly — but frame it in a way that's easy for the person to use. Rather than a blunt directive, offer it as a suggestion or option that respects their autonomy.

## The Continuum: Telling vs. Facilitating

There's a continuum from telling (on the far left) to facilitating (on the far right):

```
Telling  ------------------->  Facilitating
(negative impact)           (positive impact)
```

- **On the telling end**: You provide the answer directly. People receive the information passively. Impact is negative because it doesn't engage their thinking.
- **On the facilitating end**: You use questions and silence to help them arrive at their own conclusion. Impact is positive because it engages their brain and creates lasting change.

The further toward the facilitating end you go, the more effective the communication is for changing thinking.

## Why This Works

Neurologically, this approach works because:
- New connections form when the person themselves makes the association
- Working memory is engaged when people think actively rather than passively receive
- People remember and internalize insights they discovered themselves
