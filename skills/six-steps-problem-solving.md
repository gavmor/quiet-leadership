# Using the Six Steps for Problem Solving

> [!tldr] When someone has a problem, avoid telling them what to do. Instead, use the Six Steps to help them improve their own thinking about the problem, asking "how" questions that help them see the situation more clearly.

## The Challenge of Problem-Solving Interactions

One of the more challenging interactions leaders have with employees is when something isn't going right:
- A team needs to be replaced
- A computer system isn't working
- A launch isn't hitting its targets

The employee has not been able to work out how to solve the problem and the leader is stepping in to help.

## Standard vs. Quiet Leadership Approach

**Standard approach:** Tell people what to do. This gives quick relief but doesn't improve the person's thinking or their ability to handle similar problems in the future.

**Quiet Leadership approach:** Improve people's thinking about the problem. This takes longer but creates lasting improvement.

## The Problem Frame

The way a problem is stated (the "problem frame") determines the solutions that seem possible. Bertrand Russell noted: "The greatest challenge to any thinker is stating the problem in a way that will allow a solution."

## Using "How" Questions

One of the most effective tools in problem-solving interactions is asking "how" questions:

- "How would you know if the problem were solved?"
- "How might you approach this differently?"
- "How could you find out if that's actually the problem?"
- "How might someone else look at this situation?"

"How" questions invite people to imagine possibilities and explore the problem space more deeply.

## Avoiding Premature Solutions

A key discipline for Quiet Leaders is to resist the urge to jump to a solution. Instead:

1. **Listen for potential** - What possibilities might be hiding in what the person is saying?
2. **Reflect back** - Help them hear their own thinking more clearly
3. **Ask clarifying questions** - Especially "how" questions
4. **Be patient** - Let them work through it

## Example: David and the Team Problem

In the book, David helps an employee who had a problem with her team. Instead of telling her what to do, he asked:

- "How would you know if the team problem were solved?"
- "What if the team were working better together?"
- "How could you find out if you actually like them?"

These questions helped her reframe the problem from "I don't like the team" to "How do I get a team that works well together?" — a solvable problem with actionable steps.

## The CREATE Model for Problems

When problem-solving requires deeper exploration, use the [[create-model]] (Step 5):

1. **C**urrent reality — What's actually happening?
2. **R**esults wanted — What would success look like?
3. **E**xplored options — What could you try?
4. **A**ction plan — What will you do?
5. **T**eaching and learning — What will you learn?

## When to Use This Skill

- An employee comes to you with a problem
- A team is struggling and no one knows why
- A project isn't working out
- Someone is stuck and can't see a way forward
